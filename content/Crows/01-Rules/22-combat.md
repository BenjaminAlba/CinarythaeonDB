# Combat

> Source: Crows — Rules Booklet for Playtest 3, PDF page(s) 18–23.

Most crows don’t seek out fights, but when you’re cornered by undead in a dungeon or find a horde of blood monsters between you and a pile of gold, it’s time to draw steel. Approach combat with caution. Death is always an option.

Combat happens in rounds. During each round of combat, each combatant gets a turn to act. The round ends after all combatants have had a turn and a new one begins until all the combatants left decide or are forced to stop fighting.

## Grid and Distance

Combat uses miniatures and a grid. Distances are measured in squares on the grid. For ease of play (and defying Pythagoras), diagonal distances are measured the same as non-diagonal distances.

### Scale

Each square on the grid measures 5 feet across.

### Size and Space

Each creature has a size that describes how many squares they occupy on a grid as shown on the Creature Size table. The number of squares a creature takes up are referred to as their space.

You can’t occupy the space of another creature within 1 size category of you. You can move through the space of other creatures but can’t stop in them. Spaces occupied by enemies are difficult terrain (see Difficult Terrain).

### Squeezing

At the Ref’s discretion, a creature can squeeze themself into a space smaller than what they normally occupy. While squeezed, their speed is halved and attacks against them gain a +1 bonus.

### Reach

Your reach is determined by your size as shown on the Creature Sizes table. Your reach determines the distance in squares you can be away from a creature or object and still target it with melee attacks and other effects that specify using your reach. Humans are Medium sized and have a reach of 1. Some weapons extend your reach when you make attacks or counters with those weapons (and only when making attacks and counters with those weapons).

**Creature Sizes**

| Size | Squares | Reach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tiny | 1 | 1 |
| Small | 1 | 1 |
| Medium | 1 | 1 |
| Large | 2 × 2 | 2 |
| Huge | 3 × 3 | 2 |
| Holy Shit! | 4 × 4* | 3 |

*At the Ref’s discretion, Holy Shit! creatures can be even bigger.

## Surprised

If combat begins with one or more creatures unaware of the others, those creatures are surprised. Surprised creatures can’t take a turn and attacks against them gain an edge. After the first round of combat, surprised creatures are no longer surprised.

## Who Goes First?

At the start of each round of combat, a player of the Ref’s choice should roll 1d10. If the result is a 6 or higher, the PCs and their allies are Group A this round and their enemies are Group B, and vice versa if the result is 5 or lower. The round proceeds in the following phases:

1. Group A takes fast turns (see Fast Turn)

2. Group B takes fast turns

3. Group A takes slow turns (see Slow Turn)

4. Group B takes slow turns

You can decide to take a fast turn at any point during your group’s fast turn phase. Once play passes to the other group, you can’t interrupt the other side and take a fast turn—you’re locked in to taking a slow turn this round.

Players determine the order in which the PCs and their allies take turns within a phase, and the Ref determines the order of their enemies. You can’t break up your turn with another creature’s turn. The roll to determine Group A and Group B is repeated at the start of each round.

## Fast Turn

If you take a fast turn, you can take a maneuver or an action, but not both.

## Slow Turn

If you take a slow turn, you can take a maneuver and an action or use two maneuvers You can also choose to only take one action or one maneuver or do nothing.

## Maneuvers

The following maneuvers are available to all creatures. Some equipment or traits give you access to other maneuvers.

- **Move Speed:** You move a number of squares up to your speed. You can break up this maneuver with your action or another maneuver.

- **Shift:** Move 1 square without provoking opportunity attacks (see Opportunity Attacks).

- **Command:** You command a pet you control. See Pets in Combat.

- **Draw From Pack:** You attempt to draw an item from your backpack (see Swapping Slots).

- **Draw From Belt:** You draw an item or two from your belt (see Swapping Slots).

- **Pick Up Item:** You pick up an unattended item within your reach, provided you have enough hand slots free to grab it.

- **Dump Backpack:** You drop any number of items from your backpack onto the ground.

- **Stand Up:** You must be prone and have a speed of at least 1 to use this maneuver. You stand up.

- **Grab:** You can make the following test, targeting one creature of your size or smaller within your reach. You can only grab one creature this way at a time.

**2d10 + S**

**Tier 1:** The target can counter.

**Tier 2:** Push 1 or you can take the Shift maneuver.

**Tier 3:** The target is grabbed by you.

- **Escape Grab:** If you are grabbed, you can make the following test to wrestle your way out of the grasp of your grabber. If you are grabbed by two creatures, you take a bane on this test, or a double bane if you are grabbed by three or more creatures.

**2d10 + A or S**

**Tier 1:** The grabber can counter.

**Tier 2:** You can choose to be no longer grabbed, but if you do, the grabber can counter.

**Tier 3:** You are no longer grabbed and can move 1 square without provoking opportunity attacks.

- **Knockback:** You can make the following test, targeting one creature of your size or smaller within your reach.

**2d10 + S**

**Tier 1:** The target can counter.

**Tier 2:** Push 1

**Tier 3:** Push 2

## Actions

The following actions are available to all creatures. Some equipment or traits give you access to other actions.

- **Attack:** The most common action is to make an attack with a weapon or a spell (see Making Attacks).

- **Flee:** You can only take this action if your speed is 1 or more. You move a number of squares up to twice your speed, and enemies gain an edge on opportunity attacks made against you during this movement.

- **Ready an Action:** You can use your action to ready a specific action or maneuver that activates when a trigger you name is met. You might say, “I’m going to use my sword to attack the next enemy who moves within 1 square of me,” or, “I’m going to cast the *minor healing* spell on Boris next time he takes damage.” When the trigger you specified occurs, you can use your reaction to execute the action or maneuver. If the trigger hasn’t occurred by your next turn, you must ready the action or maneuver again to be able to use a reaction to execute it. See Reactions for more information.

- **Taunt:** Choose a creature within 10 squares of you. That creature has a bane on attacks that don’t include you as a target until the start of your next combat turn.

## Boring Stuff

Boring stuff, like opening an unlocked door or nocking an arrow, requires no maneuver or action unless the Ref deems otherwise.

## Making Shit Up

If you want to do something impactful that isn’t specified in the rules, tell the Ref. Your Ref decides if the task requires a maneuver or an action and what, if any, test needs to be made. Thinking outside the box is how many crows survive combat!

## Making Attacks

There are two kinds of attacks: melee attacks and ranged attacks, which can be made with either weapons or spells. All attacks are tests. When you make an attack, it must be made with a weapon or spellbook you are wielding. That equipment tells you if the attack is melee or ranged, what sort of test is required, and how much damage it deals at each tier to its target.

When you make an attack with a weapon (also called a weapon attack), you can apply the appropriate weapon expertise and spend Effort to improve the test result. When you make an attack with a spell (also called a spell attack), you can apply the appropriate spellcasting expertise and spend Effort.

When you get a tier 1 result on an attack, you deal no damage or other effects to your target, and there’s another potential consequence depending on if the attack was melee or ranged. A tier 1 result on an attack is called a miss. A tier 2 or 3 result on an attack is called a hit.

Thrown weapons, like daggers, allow you to make a melee or ranged attack. When you make an attack with this weapon, you decide before you make the test if it is melee or ranged.

### Melee Attacks

To make a melee attack against a creature or object, they must be within reach of the weapon or spell you wield. If you miss, the target of the attack can counter (see Counter).

All creatures can make an unarmed strike as the following melee attack. Unarmed strikes include punching, kicking, headbutting, and the like and attacks made with objects that aren’t normally used as weapons (such as torches or books). Fragile objects, such as lanterns, break after being used as a weapon in an attack.

**2d10 + A or S**

**Tier 1:** The target can counter.

**Tier 2:** 1 + A or S dam

**Tier 3:** 2 + A or S dam

### Ranged Attacks

To make a ranged attack against a creature or object, they must be within a number of squares equal to the range of the weapon or spell you wield. A ranged weapon attack can go further, and takes a -2 penalty for every 1 square the target is beyond normal range. Weapons with only a melee range are technically ranged 0.

If you miss on a ranged attack and the target was adjacent to one or more allies, roll any die. On an odd result, the attack hits one of those allies chosen randomly by the Ref. You deal the tier 2 damage of the weapon you made the attack with to that ally.

If your rolled a doom on a ranged attack while at least one ally is adjacent to your target, you hit one of those allies, randomly chosen by the Ref, and deal the tier 3 damage of the weapon.

If you make a ranged attack against an adjacent creature, the attack takes a bane.

Ammunition used in a ranged attack is destroyed. Thrown weapons can be recovered.

### Improvised Weapons

Improvised weapons deal damage as if they are unarmed attacks. At the Ref’s discretion, nonweapon objects that resemble a weapon, like the way a crowbar is similar to a hammer, can deal damage as if they are that weapon, without any special weapon qualities, however, the wielder takes a bane on such attacks with the item.

### Crits on Attacks

When you get a crit on an attack, you get an another action that you must use immediately if this action is gained on a turn other than yours. If this action is gained on your turn, you can use it at any time during the turn it was gained.

### Multiple Targets

Unless noted otherwise, an attack targets one creature or object within its reach or range. If an attack can target more than one creature or object, you choose which creatures to target and make one attack that applies to all targets. Any bonuses, penalties, edges, or banes apply that apply individual targets apply only to those targets, which could make the result of a single roll different for each. You can’t target the same creature more than once with an attack.

### Flanking

When you and one or more allies are adjacent to the same enemy and on opposite sides of the enemy, you are flanking that enemy. While flanking an enemy, you gain an edge on melee attacks against them.

You can’t flank an enemy if you or the ally you’re flanking with are prone, grabbed, or grabbing a creature who isn’t the target.

If you’re unsure whether you and an ally are flanking a foe, imagine a line extending from the center of your space to the center of your ally’s space. If that line passes through opposite sides or corners of the enemy’s space, then you and your ally are flanking the enemy.

### High Ground

If you are at least 1 square above a target, you gain an edge on attacks against them.

## Reactions

Every round, each creature has 1 reaction they can use at any point on their turn or another creature’s turn. Each reaction has a trigger and can only occur when that trigger happens. A reaction always happens after the triggering event resolves. There are two common reactions, counters and opportunity attacks, available to all creatures. Some equipment or traits give a creature access to other reactions. If you get a tier 1 result on an opportunity attack, the target can’t counter you.

### Counter

When a creature or object within reach of a melee weapon you are currently wielding makes a melee attack against you, uses the Grab or Knockback maneuver while targeting you, or attempts to escape being grabbed by you with the Escape Grab maneuver and gets a tier 1 result, you can use your reaction to counter attack, taking advantage of the opening they gave you. If you do, you deal the tier 2 result of a melee weapon you’re wielding to the triggering creature or object. If the triggering test result was doom, you deal the tier 3 result instead. If a creature making an opportunity attack against you gets a tier 1 result, you can’t counter them.

### Fighting Defensively

Counters are this game’s way of fighting defensively. You’ll never score big damage, but if you or your foes are suffering a lot of penalties to attacks, standing back and waiting to counter might be the way to survive a little longer.

### Opportunity Attack

When a creature moves out of your reach (unmodified by a weapon), you can use a reaction to make an attack with a weapon you are wielding against them before they leave your reach. This is called an opportunity attack.

## Movement

The distance you move in a single maneuver or action can’t be greater than your speed. Most of the time when a creature is moving in combat, they can use the Move Speed maneuver to move a number of squares up to a number of squares equal to their speed. Easy stuff.

### Difficult Terrain

Brambles, rubble, and other obstacles are difficult terrain, which is hard to move through. Entering a square of difficult terrain costs an extra square of movement. You can’t jump out of difficult terrain or use the Shift maneuver to enter or while within difficult terrain.

### Swimming and Climbing

Entering a square that requires you to swim, such as a lake or an ocean, or climb, such as moving up a cliff or castle wall, costs an extra square of movement. At the Ref’s discretion, you might also have to make a test to see if it’s even possible for you to swim or climb. If a creature has a swimming or climbing speed, then they can swim and climb at that speed without spending extra movement or needing to make a test, except for climbing upside down on ceilings. If a creature can climb upside down without needing to make a test, “(U)” will be included in their speed entry. If you are fully submerged in liquid and don’t have a swimming speed, you take a bane on Agility and Strength tests.

### Flying

If a creature has a flying speed, then they can move vertically and remain the air. If a creature who is flying is knocked prone or their speed is reduced to 0, they fall.

### Jumping

Whenever you move, you can jump as part of that movement. The total distance you move, including jumping, can’t exceed your speed. You make the following test when you jump. You gain an edge on this test if you move at least 2 squares on the same turn before jumping.

**2d10 + A or S**

**Tier 1:** You jump 0 squares.

**Tier 2:** You jump up to a distance of 2 squares with a height of 1 square.

**Tier 3:** You can jump up to a distance equal to 2 + your Agility or Strength (minimum total distance 3 squares) with a height of 1 square.

### Teleporting

When you teleport, you move from one space to another instantaneously, bypassing obstacles.

- Teleporting doesn’t provoke opportunity attacks or other effects triggered by moving.

- When you teleport yourself, you must have line of effect to your destination space. When you teleport another creature, you must have line of effect from the space the teleported creature leaves and to their destination space.

- A teleporting creature’s destination space can’t be occupied by another creature or object.

- The effect that lets a creature teleport indicates how far they can teleport. That distance can be greater than the creature’s speed.

- If you teleport while prone, you remain prone.

- If you teleport while grabbed, the condition ends for you. If you teleport while grabbing another creature, the condition ends for them.

## Pets in Combat

In combat and other situations tracked in combat rounds, you could have one or more pets you own, such as a horse you ride or a goat who follows you around. These pets don’t take turns of their own, but you can use the Command maneuver to have a pet you own use a maneuver and an action or two maneuvers they can perform. The Ref has final say in what maneuvers or actions an animal can or can’t take.

If you want a pet you own to do something complex or particularly dangerous, walk a complicated path or leap over a bonfire, the Ref can ask you to make the following test:

**2d10 + M**

**Tier 1:** The pet refuses the command.

**Tier 2:** The pet follows the command but is weakened after doing so.

**Tier 3:** The pet follows the command.

Commanding a pet to attack a dangerous creature typically doesn’t trigger this test, since most animals raised or purchased by crows have been trained to face monsters.

You can ride a pet you own as long as it is at least one size greater than you.

If an owner dies or is unable to command their pet or the owner takes the Flee action without commanding the pet, the pet runs away from any mortal danger that faces them, fighting if blocked from getting away.

## Forced Movement

Some weapons, spells, and other effects allow you to forcibly move a creature or object. There are two types of forced movement.

- **Push X:** The creature moves the target up to X squares away from them in a straight line, without moving them vertically. Each square the creature moves the target must put the target further away from them.

- **Slide X:** The creature moves the target up to X squares in any direction, except for vertically. A slide doesn’t need to be a straight line.

When you force move a target, you can move them fewer squares than the number indicated. Forced movement ignores difficult terrain and never provokes opportunity attacks or other reactions or effects that involve a creature moving unless specified. There is one exception. When you force move a target into damaging terrain or into terrain that produces an effect, including a temporary magic effect, they are affected as if they had moved into it willingly.

A creature can’t be force moved into a space occupied by an object or another creature who is within 1 size category of them. You can’t use a mundane weapon or effect to force move a creature larger than you. Magical forced movement effects work on all creatures.

### Vertical

If a forced movement effect has the word “vertical” in front of it, then the forced movement can move a target up or down in addition to horizontally. If a creature who can’t fly is left in midair at the end of a vertical forced move, they fall. Forced movement made against a creature who is flying is always a vertical forced move, whether or not the effect specifies it.

Non-vertical forced movement can move a target along a physical slope such as a hill or staircase. For a target to be force moved along a slope, each square of the slope can be no more than 1 square higher or lower than the previous square.

### Into a Fall

If you can’t fly and are force moved across an open space that would cause you to fall, you continue moving the total distance you were moved first. If you are still in a position to fall when the forced movement ends, you fall.

### Prepare for Battle

Rushing head-long into combat is a bad idea in most situations that will result in wounded or dead crows. It is a much better idea to prepare the battlefield with traps, choke points, high ground, hiding spots, or other environmental features and lure your foes into a place where you have the advantage. Of course if you can sneak by, scare, or distract your foes so you don’t need to risk a fight at all, that’s often the best course of action.

## Toppling Objects

Dungeons contain statues, bookshelves, metal chests, and all manner of heavy objects that can be dropped on monsters.

When you drop an object on a creature that is their size or larger, the object does 1d10 dam to the creature plus an additional 2d10 dam for each size category the object is larger than the creature. A Huge object dropped on a Medium creature deals 5d10 dam. At the Ref’s discretion, a Holy Shit object can deal even more damage, given that there is no upper limit to the size of an object in this category.

Dropping giant objects onto other creatures is often accomplished with ropes, pulleys, crowbars, or pet elephants.

You can topple objects smaller than you with your brute strength by using a maneuver. You can attempt to topple an object of your size or up to two size categories larger than you with nothing but your brute strength with the following test. The test uses an action and takes a bane if the object is one size category larger than you or a double bane if it is two size categories larger than you.

**2d10 + S**

**Tier 1:** The object doesn’t move.

**Tier 2:** You can topple the object, but if you do, you take 1d6 P dam.

**Tier 3:** The object topples.
